# Build a bridge to the exact concept

Before choosing an analogy, identify the concept's invariant: what must stay true for the explanation to be correct. Useful invariants include a direction of cause, a constraint, a ratio, a conserved quantity or a mathematical definition.

## Mapping sheet

Use this internally, or show it as a small table when the mapping itself helps:

| Familiar element | Target concept | Relation preserved | Where the mapping stops |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A specific, demonstrated anchor | The exact term being taught | The mechanism or mathematical relationship | The assumption that would make the analogy fail |

Avoid a story whose only similarity is that both systems contain “flow,” “energy,” “balance,” or “competition.” Map the relation, not just the nouns. Do not claim an illustrative analogy proves a theorem.

## Choose the smallest effective bridge

- **Unfamiliar terminology:** pair the learner's wording with the standard term and a discriminating example.
- **Missing mechanism:** explain what changes, what causes the change, and what constraint acts on it.
- **Difficult notation:** translate each symbol into a quantity already present in the example, then rebuild the relation.
- **Reasoning gap:** isolate the missing assumption or intermediate step rather than adding more metaphors.
- **Misconception:** show a simple case where the learner's current rule makes a different prediction, then repair the rule.
- **Advanced learner:** state the formal object first and use examples only where they resolve ambiguity.

## Example — derivative through travel

If the learner knows travel distance and time, average speed over a trip can introduce a rate of change. Then distinguish that interval average from instantaneous speed: shrink the interval around the time of interest and discuss the limit when it exists. Name distance as a function of time and connect its derivative to instantaneous velocity in a chosen direction.

The distinction matters: total distance divided by total time is an average, not generally an instantaneous value. Keep distance/speed and signed position/velocity distinct when the problem requires direction. A car dashboard is an illustration, not a proof of differentiability.

## Transfer and withdrawal

After the travel example, use a different quantity changing over time, such as water volume in a tank. Ask what a positive or negative rate means, or provide the worked answer if the learner does not want questions. Then express the relation with standard notation so they can recognize it outside the original story.

A good bridge helps the learner make a new prediction and then becomes less necessary. Familiar language alone is not evidence that learning occurred.
